### First Person Project Proof Review

The meeting focused on discussing the first proof to build for the First Person Project, with the team reviewing ADR001, a community-anchored proof. The scenario involves Nia proving she has a working relationship with a community member without exposing sensitive information. The Berkeley team, including Sanjam and Arka, was asked to evaluate whether proving something about a third party's credential fits within their framework and if ADR001 is the appropriate first proof to implement.

### Verifiable Trust Agents Implementation Discussion

The team discussed implementing a system using Verifiable Trust Agents (VTAs) and Verifiable Trust Communities (VTCs) to address community verification needs, particularly motivated by Linux kernel use cases. Sanjam explained their approach involves using a community agent to verify vouching processes while maintaining privacy through zero-knowledge proofs, though this requires communication with the agent and sacrifices some privacy. Mitchell clarified the terminology around VTAs and VTCs, confirming that all node communications in the trust graph involve VTA-to-VTA interactions using trust tests, with VTAs able to act as either verifiers or witnesses based on their relationship roles.

### Framework Status and Membership Credentials

Mitchell led a discussion on the status update of a framework, focusing on which parts generalize beyond personhood and implications for credential replacement with general membership. Arka explained that the initial "proofs of personhood" paper focused on credentials and VRCs but did not model community membership, which is being addressed in follow-up work. The conversation then shifted to discussing mandatory credential objects and predicates, with Mitchell highlighting the need for membership credentials and VRCs, and seeking comments on including selective disclosure and freshness policies in the specification.

### Freshness and Selective Disclosure Terms

Mitchell discussed two terms for potential inclusion in the specification: freshness and selective disclosure. He explained that freshness refers to the diminishing returns of information after a presentation, while selective disclosure involves sharing a portion of a private graph rather than the entire Maintainer Verifiable Trust community. Sanjam clarified that zero-knowledge proofs can provide both freshness and selective disclosure properties in the strongest sense, with the efficiency depending on the specific setting and statement of interest.

### Pseudonym System Linkability Challenges

The discussion focused on pseudonym systems and their limitations, particularly regarding linkability issues when the same two people interact in the same context. Arka explained that while the current system derives consistent pseudonyms for interacting parties within the same context, this could potentially allow users to link multiple interactions if they occur in the same context. Mitchell and Drummond explored scenarios involving offline verification and trust community credentials, with Mitchell asking about the sufficiency of directed identifier reuse versus the need for issuance time linkage proofs.

### VTC Signature Verification Process

The discussion focused on how VTCs handle verification of signatures when members are offline. Arka explained that in the non-privacy-preserving version, members can collect signatures from three other people, and once verified by the VTC's VTA, the signers no longer need to be online. Mitchell confirmed this approach makes sense for the PTA farm infrastructure and mentioned work on a ZKP spec repository that includes different types of proof records extracted from a paper. The conversation briefly touched on the collaborative aspect of the ZKPs documentation before being paused.

### ZKP Construction Options Discussion

The team discussed ZKP construction options for the TrustGraph specification, including custom implementations versus off-the-shelf solutions like ProofKit. Sanjam and Arka explained that when teams control their cryptographic components, they can build more efficient custom solutions, while ProofKit offers client-side proofing with server offloading for privacy on weak devices. The group identified that signature scheme choices are critical and must be made before selecting ZK proofs, with post-quantum resistance being a key consideration. Mitchell requested team members review the radar structure asynchronously and provide feedback, while also noting a request from Berkeley for a follow-up discussion on their new Linux kernel use case paper once it's ready.
